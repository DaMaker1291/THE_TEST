Five things that are genuinely unusual

1. It refuses to rush pupils

The school says it plainly: "we do not accelerate our pupils by entering them early for public examinations. Instead we stretch them by providing extra depth."

In practice: four A Levels, all taught over two years, no AS exams at all, no early GCSEs. This is why interviewers push past the syllabus instead of testing memory.

2. It owns real research equipment

The Physics department has its own scanning electron microscope — a piece of kit most universities would be pleased with. It is used in lessons and sits at the centre of the pupil research group, the Halley Research Community.

The school also runs the UK team for the International Young Physicists' Tournament.

3. It is still governed by a 900-year-old City guild

John Colet handed the school to the Mercers' Company in 1509, and it is still the governing body. That is why the school has "Apposition": it began as the guild's inspection of whether the teachers were any good.

It had teeth. Two headmasters were sacked at it — one in 1559, one in 1748 for allegedly threatening to "pull the Surmaster by the nose and kick him about the school".

4. Nearly 100 societies, many started by pupils

They meet in the long lunch break. The AI Society was started by a pupil. So when they ask "what would you start here?", it is a real question, not a hypothetical.

5. Maths is the centre of gravity

Over 85% of the year take Maths A Level and about 45% take Further Maths. There are 27 maths teachers. Corridor whiteboards have problems on them.

His PhD
London School of Economics, 2008. Title: Mixing Graph Colourings. Supervisor: Jan van den Heuvel.
His subject
Graph colouring. Imagine dots joined by lines; colour the dots so joined dots differ. His question: can you get from one valid colouring to another by changing one dot at a time, without ever breaking the rule? And how many steps does it take?
His papers
"Connectedness of the Graph of Vertex-Colourings" (2005); "Finding Paths between Graph Colourings" (2009, Theoretical Computer Science), which proved the problem is PSPACE-complete for four or more colours — meaning there is no known fast general method; and "Finding Paths Between 3-Colourings" (Journal of Graph Theory, 2011).
The conjecture
Cereceda's conjecture is still open. In plain terms: for a sparse network, if you allow two colours more than the minimum, can you always get between any two colourings in a number of steps proportional to the square of the size of the network? His own thesis showed that with only one extra colour, the number of steps can explode exponentially. That gap is the conjecture.

Why this is your best opening

Almost no 16+ candidate knows any of this. If he interviews you, you can say: "I read about Cereceda's conjecture — the quadratic bound for recolouring sparse graphs. Is it still open?" That changes the conversation, because it shows you read about him, not about the school.

Be ready to back it up: colourings are the dots of a new network, two colourings are joined if they differ in one place, and you are asking how far apart they are.

What he will probably ask

Combinatorics, not algebra. A counting puzzle (how many ways to colour a cube), a "can you always…?" question where the answer is no and you must find the case that breaks it, or a small network problem where the obvious greedy approach fails. He studies worst cases, so "find where this breaks" is his instinct.

Avoid

Don't claim to have read his paper. Don't recite the four-colour theorem. Don't call computer science "just coding" in front of a man who proves complexity results about it.

Sketch a quadratic and mark its turning point
Reported · first question

They are testing whether you can produce a curve and justify its shape, not whether you remember a formula. Take something like y = x² − 4x + 1.

Method, out loud: complete the square. y = (x−2)² − 3. So the turning point is (2, −3), it opens upwards, and the y-intercept is 1.

Then say what the completed square tells you: the bracket is never negative, so −3 is the minimum. That one sentence is the difference between drawing and understanding.

If they give you the equation already factorised, the roots are where it crosses; if in completed-square form, the turning point is immediate. Recognise which form you have been handed.

Sketch a cubic
Reported · second question

Shape first, then detail. For a positive x³ term: comes up from the bottom left, goes down to top right, and has at most three roots and two turning points.

Use y = x(x−1)(x+1) = x³ − x: roots at −1, 0, 1; crosses at 0; turning points between them. Say "it must cross the x-axis at least once, because a cubic is continuous and goes from −∞ to +∞".

Trap: a cubic with a repeated root (y = x²(x−1)) only touches at zero. Mention that and you have shown you know more than the shape.

Sketch x = y² − 3y + 2
Reported · third question, and the discriminating one

It is a parabola lying on its side, opening towards positive x — because y is squared and x is not. Most candidates draw it the wrong way round.

Factorise: x = (y−1)(y−2), so it crosses the y-axis at y = 1 and y = 2. The vertex is halfway, at y = 1.5, giving x = −0.25. So the whole curve sits to the right of x = −0.25, extending in the +x direction.

What they are checking: whether you can swap the roles of x and y without panicking. Say "x is now the output" and the sketch follows.

Solve (x² + 4x + 3)(x² + 4x + 4) = 1
Reported · fourth question, the hard one

Do not expand — that gives a quartic. Substitute. Let v = x² + 4x + 3, so the second bracket is v + 1:

v(v + 1) = 1, so v² + v − 1 = 0 — the equation that defines the golden ratio.

v = (−1 ± √5)/2, i.e. about 0.618 or −1.618.

Case 1: x² + 4x + 3 = 0.618, so x² + 4x + 2.382 = 0 and x = −2 ± √1.618. Two real roots, about −0.73 and −3.27.

Case 2: x² + 4x + 3 = −1.618, so x² + 4x + 4.618 = 0. The discriminant is negative, so no real roots.

Answer: two real solutions, x = −2 ± √((1+√5)/2).

The whole question is one idea: spot the repeated x² + 4x and substitute. Say that idea out loud before you touch the algebra.

Physics — the sheet of eight

The reported format: a single sheet, the first three questions multiple choice on forces, then longer ones. The candidate was asked to state Newton's laws when he stalled. A second candidate had a practical at the end and five questions on a sheet.

A 1 N force acts on an object that weighs 1 N. What is its acceleration?
Reported · first question on the sheet

This is the trap, and it is deliberate: 1 N is a weight, not a mass.

Weight = mg, so with g = 10, m = 1/10 = 0.1 kg. Then a = F/m = 1/0.1.

Answer: 10 m/s²

Say the distinction out loud: "its weight is 1 N, so its mass is 0.1 kg". That sentence is the whole question, and it is the most common slip in the room.

State Newton's three laws
Reported · the rescue question when a candidate froze

Have these word-perfect, because they are used as a lifeline:

  1. An object remains at rest, or moves at constant velocity, unless a resultant force acts on it.
  2. The resultant force equals mass times acceleration (F = ma) — more generally, force is the rate of change of momentum.
  3. If A exerts a force on B, B exerts an equal and opposite force on A — and the two act on different bodies.

That last clause is what separates a good answer from a recited one: a book on a table has weight and normal contact as a pair only if you are careful about which body each acts on.

The practical: you are handed apparatus and asked to find something
Reported · five questions, candidate reached two

Reported as a practical element at the end of the physics interview. The sheet asks a sequence, not one answer. Whatever the apparatus, the five moves are the same:

  1. What are you measuring, and with what? Name the instrument and its resolution.
  2. What is the relationship? State the equation you will plot, and therefore what the gradient means.
  3. Which variables? Independent, dependent, and — the one people forget — what you hold constant.
  4. What could go wrong? Parallax, zero error, heating, friction, reaction time, non-uniform wire.
  5. How would you improve it? Repeat and average, use a larger range, use a set square, use a micrometer.

Worked example: find the spring constant. Hang masses, measure extension with a ruler (±1 mm), plot F against x; the gradient is k. Control: same spring, no over-stretching past the elastic limit. Error: reading the bottom of the masses at eye level. Improve: add masses in steps and repeat, take the gradient rather than one reading.

I think of a number between 1 and 100. You can only ask "higher or lower". How many guesses do you need?
Reported · first question of the computer science interview

Seven. Each question halves the remaining range, so you need the smallest n with 2ⁿ ≥ 100. Since 2⁶ = 64 and 2⁷ = 128, the answer is 7.

Say the name: binary search. Say the condition: it only works because the list is ordered. Say the cost: doubling the range adds one guess — that is why it is O(log n).

This is the easiest question of the day and the easiest to over-answer. Give the number, the reason, then stop.

A ten-digit number: the first digit says how many 0s it contains, the second how many 1s, and so on. Find it.
Reported · the hard follow-up

It is a known puzzle — an "autobiographical" or self-descriptive number — and there is exactly one with ten digits: 6210001000.

Check it: six 0s, two 1s, one 2, zero 3s, 4s, 5s, one 6, and no 7s, 8s or 9s.

How to find it under pressure: the digits must sum to 10 (they count ten positions), which forces most digits to be small. Try putting 6 in the first place because a ten-digit number like this usually has many zeros; then two 1s and one 2 and one 6 follow, and the rest are zeros. Say what you are assuming as you go, because the method is the mark.

Why learn C when Python and Java exist?
Reported · discussion question

Because C shows you the machine: manual memory management teaches you what a pointer, the stack and the heap actually are, which is why operating systems, embedded firmware and language runtimes are still written in it. It also makes every higher-level language stop being magic.

Earn the last mark with the caveat: you would not choose C for a web app, because safety and speed of development matter — which is exactly why Rust exists.

The candidate who reported this answered "it is old but still widely used for security" and got in. A modest, correct answer delivered clearly beats a clever one delivered hesitantly.

If physics is your weak subject

Physics, from shaky to survivable in four days

You are not being asked to know GCSE physics. You are being asked about eight things, on a sheet, out loud. This page teaches those eight things and gives you the sentences to say while you use them.

Self-test. Answers are hidden and the clock starts when you switch on. Work each one on paper, then reveal the worked answer and mark yourself honestly.

00:00 0 revealed

Why this is winnable. The reported physics interview was a sheet of eight questions: three multiple-choice on forces, then longer ones, plus a practical at the end. A second candidate reached two questions and still got through the day. Nobody is expecting a finished script — they are watching how you move when you are unsure.

What actually came up

  • A 1 N force on an object that weighs 1 N — the weight-versus-mass trap.
  • Being asked to state Newton's three laws when the candidate stalled. This is the rescue question, so it is the one thing you must know word-perfect.
  • Multiple-choice forces questions.
  • A practical at the end, with a five-question sheet: variables, readings, what could go wrong.

The eight things to learn

1. Weight is not mass

Mass is kilograms — how much stuff. Weight is newtons — the pull of gravity on that stuff. W = mg, and in this interview g = 10.

Say: "Its weight is 1 N, so its mass is 0.1 kg."

Trap: 1 N is never 1 kg. Divide by 10.

2. F = ma, and "resultant"

The acceleration depends on the resultant force — what is left after opposing forces cancel.

Say: "Resultant force is 5 N, mass 0.5 kg, so a = F/m = 10 m/s²."

Trap: using the applied force when friction or weight opposes it.

3. Newton's three laws, word-perfect

  1. An object stays at rest, or moves at constant velocity, unless a resultant force acts on it.
  2. F = ma: resultant force equals mass times acceleration.
  3. If A pushes B, B pushes A with an equal and opposite force — on a different body.

Trap: forgetting "different body". Weight and normal contact on a book are not a third-law pair.

4. Energy is conserved, not used up

Energy moves between stores: kinetic, gravitational, elastic, thermal, chemical. KE = ½mv² and GPE = mgh.

Say: "The gravitational store empties into the kinetic store, ignoring air resistance."

Trap: saying energy is "lost". It is transferred to the surroundings, usually thermally.

5. Circuits: V = IR

Series: current the same everywhere, voltages add. Parallel: voltage the same, currents add. Power P = VI = I²R.

Say: "Two resistors in parallel have a smaller combined resistance than either, because there are more paths."

Trap: adding parallel resistances directly. Use 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂.

6. Motion: the four equations

v = u + at · s = ut + ½at² · v² = u² + 2as · s = (u+v)t/2

Say: "I'll list what I know: u, v, a, t, s — then pick the equation missing the thing I don't have."

Trap: using them when acceleration is not constant. Free fall and braking are fine; a bouncing ball is not.

7. Graphs: gradient and area

On a velocity–time graph, the gradient is acceleration and the area underneath is distance. On a distance–time graph, the gradient is speed.

Say: "The gradient here is the acceleration, so the straight line means constant acceleration."

Trap: reading a v–t graph as if it showed the journey's shape.

8. Any experiment, five questions

Whatever the apparatus: what you measure and with what; the relationship and what the gradient means; independent, dependent, control variables; what could go wrong; how you would improve it.

Say: "I'd repeat each reading and average, because reaction time is the biggest random error."

When you don't know — the script

This is the whole skill, and it works whether or not you can do the physics:

  1. Name what is being asked. "You want the acceleration."
  2. List what you have. "I have a force of 1 N and the object's weight is 1 N."
  3. Name the equation. "That's F = ma, so I need the mass."
  4. Convert and substitute, out loud. "Weight 1 N, so mass 0.1 kg. So a = 10."
  5. Sanity-check and state the assumption. "10 m/s², about g — that seems right. I'm assuming g = 10 and no friction."

If you truly cannot start: "I don't know this one. Can I talk through what I'd try?" That sentence has never lost anyone a place; a bluff has.

Six worked questions, easiest first

A 2 kg mass hangs from a string. What is the tension?

It is not accelerating, so the tension balances the weight. W = mg = 2 × 10 = 20 N.

Answer: 20 N. Say "the forces balance because it is stationary" — that is law 1 in action.

A 5 N force acts on a 500 g trolley. What is its acceleration?

Convert first: 500 g = 0.5 kg. Then a = F/m = 5/0.5.

Answer: 10 m/s². The mark is for converting grams, not for the division.

A 2 kg mass is on a scale in a lift accelerating upwards at 2 m/s². What does the scale read?

The scale must supply the weight and the extra force to accelerate. N = m(g + a) = 2(10 + 2).

Answer: 24 N — heavier than its 20 N weight. Going down at 2 m/s² it would read 16 N, and in free fall 0 N.

A car at 20 m/s brakes to rest in 4 s. Find the acceleration and the distance.

a = (v − u)/t = (0 − 20)/4 = −5 m/s².

Distance with constant acceleration: s = (u + v)t/2 = (20 + 0) × 4/2.

Answer: −5 m/s² (a deceleration) and 40 m. Or use the area under the v–t graph — a triangle, half base times height.

3 Ω and 6 Ω in parallel across 12 V. Find the total resistance and the current in each branch.

1/R = 1/3 + 1/6 = 2/6 + 1/6 = 3/6, so R = 2 Ω.

Each branch has the full 12 V: I = V/R gives 4 A through the 3 Ω and 2 A through the 6 Ω.

Answer: 2 Ω, 4 A and 2 A, total 6 A. Check: 12/2 = 6. Always check that way.

A ball is dropped from 5 m. How fast is it going when it lands?

Energy: mgh = ½mv², so the mass cancels and v = √(2gh) = √(2 × 10 × 5) = √100.

Answer: 10 m/s. Saying "the mass cancels" is worth as much as the number — it shows you know why.

Monday 16 to Saturday 21 November

The five days, in order of what helps most

Ninety minutes a day, maximum. You are not learning physics or maths from scratch — you are practising saying what you already half-know, out loud, while someone interrupts you.

Three rules for the week. Everything is done aloud, standing up, with a pen and paper. Nothing is read silently twice. And every session ends by writing one sentence: what did I get wrong and what is the fix?

Monday — the statement, and the graphs

Session 1 (35 min): write the five statement answers. They are the fixed questions, about 100 words each:

  1. Why St Paul's? — one specific thing (a society, the Toller Prize, the electron microscope), and what you would do with it.
  2. Co-curriculars at your current school — what you do, and what you get from it, not a list.
  3. A character strength with a recent example — one incident, dated, with what you actually did.
  4. Something you find tricky and how you would improve it — a real weakness with a mechanism, not a humblebrag.
  5. One of the five values with a situation — kindness, respect, humility, integrity or resilience.

Then read all five aloud once. Every claim in them is a question you have volunteered for, so cut anything you could not evidence in the room.

Session 2 (25 min): graph sketching. Sketch, labelling intercepts and turning points, saying the method out loud: y = x² + 2x − 3; y = (x−2)³; x = y² − 4; y = 1/x. The sideways parabola is the one they actually used.

Done when: you can do the sideways parabola in under 60 seconds without hesitating about which way it opens.

Tuesday — physics rescue, part one

Session 1 (30 min): the eight things. Go to Physics rescue and work through cards 1–4 only: weight versus mass, F = ma, Newton's laws, energy. Say each "Say:" line aloud as you read it.

Session 2 (20 min): Newton's laws from memory. Write all three, twice, then say them to someone. This is the exact question an interviewer used to rescue a frozen candidate — it is the one thing that must be automatic.

Session 3 (15 min): the weight trap. Do these in your head, aloud: a 0.3 N object's mass; a 5 N force on a 500 g trolley; a 2 kg mass hanging on a string.

Done when: you never say "1 N is 1 kg", and the three laws come out in order without prompting.

Wednesday — physics rescue, part two, and a timed sheet

Session 1 (30 min): cards 5–8. Circuits, motion equations, graphs, and the five experiment questions. Do the six worked questions on the physics page on paper, then check them and mark only the unit conversions.

Session 2 (20 min): timed sheet practice. This is the format rehearsal. Take eight questions from the Questions tab, set a timer for 15 minutes, and work them on paper while narrating — out loud, to a wall or a person. Stop at 15 minutes, finished or not. The real sheet is longer than the time; the skill is being calm about that.

Session 3 (10 min): the script. Take one question you cannot do and say the five-step script anyway: what is asked, what I have, which equation, substitute, sanity-check.

Done when: you have run out of time on purpose and did not panic, and you can design an experiment using the five questions.

Thursday — maths aloud, and computing

Session 1 (35 min): the reported maths interview. Do the four reported questions in order, out loud, on paper: quadratic with turning point, cubic, x = y² − 3y + 2, and the substitution one. Then the last five minutes of the real interview: "why did you take GCSE maths early, and what do you do in maths lessons now?" Answer both properly.

Session 2 (25 min): computing, out loud. The binary search question (7 guesses, and why); the self-descriptive number 6210001000 reconstructed from scratch; "why C" in four sentences; and one project explained in 45 seconds — what it does, one design decision, one bug you fixed.

Session 3 (10 min): trace a loop on paper. Write a six-line loop, then trace it by hand and say each variable's value as it changes. If they hand you code, this is what they are watching.

Done when: you can explain your own project without saying "it's kind of hard to explain".

Friday — the mock, then stop

Session 1 (30 min): a real mock. Someone else asks you six questions from the Questions tab. Their rules: they may interrupt, ask "why?" three times, and change one number mid-question. Yours: never go silent for more than five seconds, and if you are stuck, use the script.

Session 2 (20 min): the general interview. Rehearse, aloud: why St Paul's; what challenges you would face and how you would overcome them; what makes you laugh; what motivates you; what you do in your free time. The second one is the only hard one — a real challenge plus a mechanism.

Session 3 (15 min): two questions to ask them. About their subject, not admissions. Write them down and take them with you.

Then stop. No new material after Friday lunchtime. Re-read the statement, the three laws, and the graph shapes, and go to bed.

It also fixes the department's size at “ten full time physicists” with “two full time technicians”. What it does not say: the name of his successor. No source does.
  • International Young Physicists' Tournament, UK member-organisation application, July 2025. Proves: St Paul's applied in its own name for national membership of the IYPT, and names Simon Parsons as the person committing to act as team leader, giving the 2025 tournament as his most recent participation. This is the only public evidence of who leads a physics team at the school — not proof of a head-of-department title, and the card says so.
  • IAI News, “The Philosopher and the Ocean”, 27 March 2016. Proves: Rufus Duits's own byline, which gives his title as “Head of Philosophy at St. Paul's School, London”. That is why his card calls him Head of Philosophy; the school's staff page lists him only as a member of the theology and philosophy department.
  • School news, “Three pupils invited to IMO training camp”, 11 March 2024. Proves: that the Olympiad preparation belongs to Dr Zhivko Stoyanov — the school thanks “the amazing work of Dr Zhivko Stoyanov in preparing these (and a myriad of other) boys” — and that Dr Hemery's role was to write the report. This is the source that corrects an earlier draft of this file, which credited the programme to the author rather than the teacher. Included here because a correction without its evidence is just another assertion.

What I could not confirm

  • Who is Head of Physics now. The school advertised the post for September 2025 and has never announced the appointment.
  • Background for most staff. Nine of twelve physicists have no public profile. Neither does the Head of Mathematics, beyond his own blog post.
  • Official interview questions beyond maths. Only the nine maths questions are published by the school. The physics and computing questions are candidate reports, each resting on one account, and the Further Maths arrangement is inferred from one candidate's timetable.
  • Any second account of the computing interview. I searched The Student Room, Reddit, tutor sites, school-review sites and Chinese-language forums. There is one, and it is the one used here.
  • The numerical problem-solving paper. No sample has ever been published.

Sources I read and deliberately did not use

  • BlackStone Tutors, “St Paul's School 16+ Interview Questions and Answers”. Read in full, then set aside. The questions are not St Paul's-specific: the same list appears on their pages for Highgate, Eton, Harrow, Westminster, Marlborough, Eltham, Francis Holland and LAE, all published within three days of each other in January–February 2024. The clearest proof that it is a template is their Brampton Manor page, which offers “Past Interview Questions” for a school that, in its own words, “does not routinely interview”. And their model answer to “why this school” leans on “rich history dating back to 1509” and “notable alumni” — the two things the Head of Mathematics and the High Master have both said they do not want to hear.
  • Superprof's St Paul's guide. Its 103-divided-by-2 question and its unseen-poem analysis belong to the 13+ interview, not the 16+. The same page mixes the two entry points together.
  • Keystone Tutors. Largely 13+/ISEB; its interview list is the generic “favourite subject, hobbies, why us” set.
  • Owl Tutors. Explicitly describes the 13+ interview. Its advice that boys should not be “trained” is sound, and is quoted on the interview page.

None of these is dishonest. They are marketing pages, and their incentive is to look authoritative to a parent, not to be accurate about one school's interview. Where I have used their material it is the structural detail that can be checked against the school's own pages.
